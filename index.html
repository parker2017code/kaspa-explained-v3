    01 Ten slips a second

    Kaspa is a ledger: a record of who paid whom, kept by many computers that don't trust each other. To add a page, a computer burns electricity solving a puzzle (proof of work, as in Bitcoin).

    Bitcoin adds a page about every 10 minutes1. Kaspa adds about ten a second2,3. The tape at the top is that, live.

    Bitcoin, one page
    ~600 s
    Kaspa, one page
    ~0.1 s

    02 Many tills, one roll

    At that speed, two computers often finish a page at the same moment, like two tills ringing up at once. What happens to the second slip?

    SLIP Atill 1
    SLIP Btill 2VOID
    NEXT SLIP ← points back to: A

    Each Kaspa block can point back to up to 16 earlier blocks5, so parallel slips get stapled together instead of binned. A rule called GHOSTDAG then puts every slip in one agreed order4.

    03 Try to cheat the till

    The classic scam: spend coins in public, secretly print slips where you spend them again, then reveal yours. Have a go.

    Pick a time, then reveal.

    Honest slips point back at every slip their makers have seen. Yours saw none of theirs. If more than 124 slips were made that yours never saw5, GHOSTDAG stamps yours red4.

    Red slips stay on the roll but are ordered after the honest ones, so the honest spend wins. Their maker isn't paid; whoever staples them in is6.

    After 12 hours, the order is final and nodes won't accept a rewrite7.

      1. Speed costs something. More blocks means more data for everyone checking the ledger. Watch whether lots of people keep running nodes.
